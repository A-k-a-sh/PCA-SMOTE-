%% ----- Abstract ------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
Deep latent-space oversampling methods such as DeepSMOTE have shown promise
for class-imbalanced image classification.
However, their behaviour under \emph{extreme} imbalance (minority class size
$n \leq 80$) has not been rigorously studied.
We reproduce DeepSMOTE on MNIST (imbalance ratio 100:1) and a 4-class
subset of CIFAR-10 (25:1, minority class \( n = 80 \)) and observe a systematic failure:
standard latent SMOTE applied until all classes are balanced to the majority
class count causes \emph{synthetic overfitting}, where the classifier learns the
interpolated synthetic manifold rather than the true class distribution,
degrading minority-class accuracy below the unaugmented baseline.
We further show that Fr\'{e}chet Inception Distance (FID) and classifier accuracy are
empirically \emph{decoupled} in this regime -- methods that produce more realistic
synthetic images (lower FID) can still yield \emph{worse} classifiers.
To address both failure modes, we propose \textbf{PCA-SMOTE}, which projects each
class's latent vectors to their intrinsic low-dimensional PCA subspace before
SMOTE interpolation, then projects back.
This confines interpolation to the meaningful directions of variation and
eliminates noisy high-dimensional interpolation artefacts.
Critically, we also show that the choice of synthetic target count matters as
much as the generation method itself: optimal performance is achieved at
approximately 5--6$\times$ the real sample count, not the conventional
``balance to majority'' target.
Across three random seeds on both datasets, PCA-SMOTE at the optimal target
count achieves statistically significant improvements over DeepSMOTE
($p = 0.0013$, Wilcoxon signed-rank test) and achieves a higher mean
accuracy than the unaugmented baseline on both benchmarks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Deep convolutional neural networks achieve state-of-the-art performance on image classification tasks when trained on massive, perfectly balanced datasets. In real-world applications, however, data is often heavily skewed, with majority classes dominating the dataset and critical minority classes represented by only a handful of samples. This class imbalance biases the classifier towards the majority distribution, severely degrading its ability to detect the minority class \citep{he2009learning,johnson2019survey}. 

To address this, oversampling techniques generate synthetic examples for the minority class. While SMOTE \citep{chawla2002smote} is widely successful for tabular data, linear interpolation in pixel space fails to generate semantically meaningful images. Deep latent-space oversampling methods, notably DeepSMOTE \citep{dablain2022deepsmote}, solve this by training an autoencoder to compress images into a lower-dimensional latent space, applying SMOTE to the latent vectors, and decoding them back into images. 

While these methods show promise for moderate imbalance ratios, their behaviour under \emph{extreme} class imbalance (where the minority class contains $n \le 80$ samples) remains largely unexplored. In this paper, we reproduce the DeepSMOTE architecture and evaluate it on extreme imbalance configurations of MNIST and CIFAR-10. We identify a critical failure mode we term \emph{synthetic overfitting}: when a minority class is aggressively oversampled to match the majority count, the classifier learns a noisy, artificially constructed manifold that degrades minority-class accuracy below that of an unaugmented baseline. Furthermore, we empirically demonstrate a decoupling between generative image quality (Fréchet Inception Distance, FID \citep{heusel2017gans}) and downstream classifier utility, proving that realistic synthetic images do not guarantee better classifiers.

To resolve these failures, we propose \textbf{PCA-SMOTE}. By projecting the extreme minority class into a constrained principal component subspace before applying SMOTE, we prevent interpolation through empty, high-dimensional noise. Combined with a newly proposed heuristic for selecting the optimal synthetic target count, PCA-SMOTE achieves statistically significant accuracy improvements over standard latent oversampling methods.

To facilitate full reproducibility of our failure analysis and proposed methodology, our complete source code and experimental configurations are publicly available at \url{https://github.com/A-k-a-sh/PCA-SMOTE-}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Oversampling for imbalanced data was popularized by SMOTE \citep{chawla2002smote}, which interpolates between a sample and its $k$-nearest neighbors. Because raw pixel interpolation yields blurry, ghosting artifacts, subsequent works shifted to deep generative models. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have been adapted for this purpose (e.g., BAGAN \citep{mariani2018bagan}, GAMO \citep{mullick2019gamo}), but they are notoriously difficult to train on highly imbalanced datasets due to mode collapse. Variants of SMOTE (e.g., Borderline-SMOTE \citep{han2005borderline}, ADASYN \citep{he2008adasyn}, SMOTEBoost \citep{chawla2003smoteboost}) attempted to improve tabular generation boundaries but still fail in high-dimensional image spaces.

Latent-space oversampling methods bypass GAN instability by utilizing deterministic autoencoders. DeepSMOTE \citep{dablain2022deepsmote} trains a DCGAN-based autoencoder \citep{radford2015unsupervised} with a specialized penalty loss to cluster intra-class features, allowing standard SMOTE to be applied in the latent space. While the original authors tested DeepSMOTE on substantial imbalance ratios (e.g., 400:1), their absolute minority class counts remained relatively large (e.g., $n > 200$). Our work uniquely targets the \emph{extreme} minority regime ($n \le 80$), where the latent manifold assumption breaks down.

\section{Background: DeepSMOTE}
\label{sec:background}
DeepSMOTE \citep{dablain2022deepsmote} consists of a three-phase pipeline. First, an encoder-decoder architecture is trained on the imbalanced dataset. The network uses a reconstruction loss alongside a cyclic permutation penalty term to force intra-class embeddings closer together. Second, the trained encoder projects the minority class images into a high-dimensional continuous latent space. For each minority latent vector, standard SMOTE selects a neighbor from its $k=5$ nearest neighbors and generates a synthetic vector via linear interpolation with a gap coefficient $\lambda$ drawn from $\mathcal{U}(0, 1)$. Finally, these synthetic latent vectors are passed through the frozen decoder to generate synthetic minority images, which are combined with the real data to train a classifier.

\section{Failure Analysis}
\label{sec:failure}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}
To analyze the behaviour of latent-space oversampling under extreme imbalance, we reconstruct the DeepSMOTE \citep{dablain2022deepsmote} pipeline on two benchmark datasets: MNIST \citep{lecun1998gradient} and a 4-class subset of CIFAR-10 \citep{krizhevsky2009learning} (Ship, Dog, Bird, Airplane). For MNIST, we establish a 100:1 imbalance ratio, reducing the minority class (Digit 9) to $n=40$ samples. For CIFAR-10, we reduce the ``Airplane'' class to $n=80$ samples (a 25:1 ratio against the majority class of $n=2000$). We select a 4-class subset to isolate the effect of extreme minority imbalance on a controlled classification task. We train the identical autoencoder architecture specified by the original authors (a DCGAN-based topology \citep{radford2015unsupervised}) until convergence. Figure \ref{fig:tsne} visualizes the resulting 600-dimensional latent spaces projected via t-SNE \citep{vandermaaten2008tsne}. While the majority classes form dense, well-defined clusters, the extreme minority classes are sparse and fractured, setting the stage for our failure analysis.

\subsection{Finding 1: Synthetic Overfitting}
\label{subsec:overfitting}
The conventional heuristic for SMOTE-based methods is to generate synthetic samples until all classes are perfectly balanced (i.e., matching the majority class count). We sweep the target generation count for the minority class and track the downstream classifier's performance. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:target_sweep}, increasing the number of synthetic DeepSMOTE samples initially improves minority-class accuracy. However, pushing the target count toward full balance causes a sharp degradation in performance. For instance, on CIFAR-10, generating samples up to the fully balanced target of 2000 drops the minority accuracy to 19.9\%, substantially worse than the unaugmented baseline of 23.5\%. 

We term this phenomenon \emph{synthetic overfitting}. Because the extreme minority class ($n=80$) is too sparse to form a coherent manifold in 600 dimensions, interpolating among its nearest neighbors produces redundant or noisy latent vectors. The downstream classifier learns this artificially constructed, flawed synthetic distribution rather than generalizing to the true unseen data distribution.

    \centering

    \caption{Target sweep for minority class generation on MNIST (left) and CIFAR-10 (right). DeepSMOTE performance peaks at a low synthetic ratio (approx. $5\times$ real samples) and degrades sharply as it approaches the full balance target, demonstrating synthetic overfitting.}
    \label{fig:target_sweep}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Finding 2: The FID--Accuracy Decoupling}
\label{subsec:fid}
In generative modeling, Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) \citep{heusel2017gans} is the standard metric for image quality. Previous works often assume that better synthetic images (lower FID) naturally translate to better downstream classifier performance. Our experiments in the extreme imbalance regime reveal that these two metrics can completely decouple.

As detailed in Table \ref{tab:fid_paradox}, DeepSMOTE produces visually superior synthetic samples with a significantly lower FID score (20.5 on MNIST). However, this image realism masks a failure in informational diversity. The proposed PCA-SMOTE method yields blurrier images (higher FID of 32.2) due to information loss during subspace projection, yet it produces a \emph{superior} classifier (85.6\% accuracy vs. 79.9\% for DeepSMOTE). This decoupling indicates that evaluating oversampling techniques purely on image quality metrics like FID is insufficient and potentially misleading.

\subsection{Finding 3: Latent Space Sparsity Diagnosis}
\label{subsec:sparsity}
To mathematically formalize why synthetic overfitting occurs, we compute a nearest-neighbor (NN) distance ratio diagnostic for each class in the latent space. We define this ratio as the mean $k$-NN Euclidean distance normalized by the mean vector norm of the class. A ratio approaching 1.0 indicates that nearest neighbors are as distant as random points in the space, implying an absence of local manifold structure. 

Figure \ref{fig:nn_ratio} shows this diagnostic for both datasets. The extreme minority classes exhibit NN ratios dangerously close to 1.0 (0.989 for MNIST Class 9; 0.956 for CIFAR-10 Airplane). When standard SMOTE interpolates between these distant, unstructured points in 600 dimensions, it generates vectors traversing ``empty'' space that often crosses classification boundaries. This diagnostic confirms that standard Euclidean interpolation is inherently unreliable when the local latent density is insufficient.

\end{figure}

\section{Proposed Method: PCA-SMOTE}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{subsec:motivation}
Our failure analysis demonstrated that in cases of extreme imbalance, the minority class lacks the latent density required for standard Euclidean interpolation. The 600-dimensional space is overwhelmingly empty, causing SMOTE to generate vectors with noisy, out-of-distribution features that degrade classifier performance. However, while the ambient dimension is 600, the \emph{intrinsic} dimension of the minority class's meaningful variations (e.g., rotation, scale, or lighting) is much lower. By restricting the interpolation exclusively to this intrinsic subspace, we hypothesize that we can eliminate high-dimensional noise and prevent the generation of semantically invalid samples. 

\subsection{Algorithm}
\label{subsec:algorithm}
To achieve this, we propose \textbf{PCA-SMOTE}. Instead of interpolating directly in the full latent space, PCA-SMOTE projects the minority class into a lower-dimensional Principal Component Analysis (PCA) subspace, performs SMOTE within this constrained manifold, and then projects the synthetic samples back to the original space for decoding. The pipeline is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:pcasmote_pipeline} and proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Subspace Projection:} Let $Z_{min} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ be the encoded latent vectors of the minority class, where $d=600$. We apply PCA \citep{pearson1901pca} to $Z_{min}$ and retain the top $k$ principal components (where $k \ll d$). The data is projected into this subspace, yielding $Z'_{min} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$. In our experiments, setting $k=20$ captures the majority of the variance.
    \item \textbf{Subspace Interpolation:} Standard SMOTE is applied within the $k$-dimensional space. For a sample $z'_i \in Z'_{min}$ and its nearest neighbor $z'_j$, a synthetic sample is generated as:
    $z'_{syn} = z'_i + \lambda (z'_j - z'_i)$ where $\lambda \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1)$.
    \item \textbf{Inverse Projection:} The synthetic vectors $Z'_{syn}$ are projected back into the original 600-dimensional space using the inverse PCA transform, yielding $Z_{syn} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$.
    \item \textbf{Decoding:} Finally, $Z_{syn}$ is passed through the pre-trained decoder to generate the final synthetic images.
\end{enumerate}  

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig4_pcasmote_pipeline.png}
    \caption{The PCA-SMOTE pipeline. By restricting SMOTE interpolation to the top-$k$ principal components (intrinsic subspace), we eliminate high-dimensional noise and generate structurally coherent samples.}
    \label{fig:pcasmote_pipeline}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Optimal Target Count Selection}
\label{subsec:target}
A critical corollary to our methodology is the rejection of the standard ``balance to majority'' heuristic. As established in Section \ref{sec:failure}, aggressively oversampling an extreme minority class inevitably leads to synthetic overfitting, regardless of the generation method used. 

Instead, our empirical results across both MNIST and CIFAR-10 suggest a new heuristic for extreme imbalance: the synthetic generation target should be bounded to a small multiple of the real sample count. We find that the optimal target count $T$ is approximately $5\times$ to $6\times$ the size of the minority class ($T \approx 5n_{min}$). Generating samples beyond this threshold introduces redundant information that overrides the true data distribution, causing the downstream classifier's performance to rapidly decline. PCA-SMOTE, combined with this targeted generation ratio, forms our complete proposed solution.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets and Implementation Details}
\label{subsec:impl}
We evaluate our approach on two image classification benchmarks: MNIST \citep{lecun1998gradient} and a 4-class subset of CIFAR-10 \citep{krizhevsky2009learning} (Ship, Dog, Bird, Airplane). While previous works evaluated DeepSMOTE under moderate absolute minority counts (e.g., $n \ge 400$), we establish an extreme imbalance regime to stress-test the latent manifold. For MNIST, we use a 100:1 imbalance ratio, reducing Class 9 to $n=40$ samples. For CIFAR-10, we establish a 25:1 ratio, reducing the Airplane class to $n=80$ samples. 

For downstream classification, we intentionally train a lightweight Convolutional Neural Network (SmallCNN) \citep{lecun1989backpropagation} from scratch using the Adam optimizer \citep{kingma2014adam}. This isolates the direct impact of the synthetic oversampling distribution and avoids confounding variables introduced by heavy architectures (like ResNet-18 \citep{krizhevsky2012imagenet}) or ImageNet pre-training priors. Because global metrics like overall accuracy are insensitive to extreme minority classes, we report the absolute classification accuracy of the target minority class. All experiments are independently repeated across three random seeds (10, 20, and 60) to ensure statistical robustness.

\subsection{MNIST Results}
\label{subsec:mnist}
Table \ref{tab:mnist_results} presents the minority class accuracy across three random seeds on the MNIST dataset. Standard DeepSMOTE, when generating samples to achieve full balance ($T=4000$), consistently underperforms the unaugmented baseline (mean accuracy drops from 87.8\% to 82.1\%). This confirms the synthetic overfitting diagnosis discussed in Section \ref{sec:failure}.

Applying PCA-SMOTE while maintaining the full balance target ($T=4000$) partially recovers the performance (86.6\% mean), proving that subspace interpolation successfully removes harmful high-dimensional noise. Finally, combining PCA-SMOTE with the optimal target generation count ($T \approx 200$, or $5\times$ the real sample count) achieves the highest performance (88.8\% mean), exceeding the baseline in all seeds.

\subsection{Statistical Validation}
\label{subsec:stats}

To validate our empirical observations, we conducted non-parametric Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests \citep{wilcoxon1945individual} across multiple random seeds and datasets (CIFAR-10 and MNIST).
First, we confirmed that standard latent-space oversampling (DeepSMOTE) statistically
degrades minority-class performance compared to the unaugmented imbalanced baseline
at extreme imbalance ratios ($p = 0.0020$).
Second, evaluating across all paired hyperparameter and seed configurations ($n = 15$),
our proposed PCA-SMOTE method significantly outperformed standard DeepSMOTE ($p = 0.0013$),
validating the efficacy of intrinsic subspace interpolation in sparse,
highly-imbalanced latent manifolds.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The failure of standard latent interpolation under extreme imbalance highlights a flaw in assuming Euclidean spaces smoothly map to semantic image manifolds. When a class has only 40 to 80 samples, a 600-dimensional encoded space is overwhelmingly sparse. Interpolating between two distant neighbors traverses regions of the latent space that do not correspond to the true data distribution. 

PCA-SMOTE solves this by recognizing that the \emph{intrinsic} dimensionality of a class's structural variation is much lower than the ambient dimension of the autoencoder's bottleneck. By projecting into the top principal components, we discard the empty ambient dimensions that cause noisy generation. Furthermore, our discovery that the optimal target count is merely $5\times$ the real sample count challenges the longstanding oversampling heuristic of perfectly balancing class sizes, suggesting that generative methods should prioritize sample diversity over absolute class parity.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
In this paper, we evaluated deep latent-space oversampling techniques under conditions of extreme class imbalance ($n \le 80$). We identified \emph{synthetic overfitting} as a critical failure mode where aggressive oversampling degrades classifier performance below an unaugmented baseline. We also empirically demonstrated that Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) does not reliably correlate with downstream classifier utility in this regime. To address these issues, we proposed PCA-SMOTE, an algorithm that restricts SMOTE interpolation to the intrinsic low-dimensional subspace of the minority class. Coupled with an optimal target generation heuristic, PCA-SMOTE successfully mitigated synthetic overfitting and achieved statistically significant performance improvements on both MNIST and CIFAR-10. Future work will explore non-linear dimensionality reduction (such as UMAP \citep{mcinnes2018umap}) within the latent space prior to interpolation.
